Not a legal opinion. This page is a search launcher, not a legal clearance, novelty, patentability or freedom-to-operate opinion. It doesn't analyze results, and finding nothing doesn't mean nothing exists. Talk to a registered patent attorney or agent (or a trademark attorney) before making filing or launch decisions.

How to do a basic prior-art search

Prior art is anything made public before your filing date that describes the same or a similar idea: patents, published applications, papers, products, websites, videos and code.

1. Break the idea into features

Write down the problem, the solution and the three to five technical features that make your version different. Search each feature, not just the product name.

2. Build a keyword list

List synonyms and alternative terms (“robot”, “autonomous device”, “apparatus”). Patents often use formal, generic words. Try British and American spellings.

3. Use classifications

When you find a close patent, note its CPC or IPC classification codes and search within those classes on Espacenet or Google Patents to find related documents.

4. Follow citations

Check the “cited by” and “citing” lists on relevant patents and papers. Citation trails often lead to the closest art quickly.

5. Search beyond patents

Look at papers, theses, product listings, archived websites and open-source code. For brands, search trademark registers before choosing a name or logo.

6. Record what you searched

Keep the date, database, query and the most relevant results. Download the search log above, and save copies of key documents. Share this with your attorney.

Useful search tricks. Put exact phrases in quotes (“cleaning brush”). Use OR for synonyms (robot OR drone). On Google Patents you can add filters after searching, such as dates, countries and CPC codes. Many databases also support wildcards like clean*.

Keep your idea confidential. Search queries go to third-party websites under their own terms. Use general technical keywords, not your full invention description, and record your work privately (for example with the Hash & Proof Tool) before sharing details.
